Overview

I built a cloth simulator with a spring grid, Verlet integration, and collisions with planes, spheres, and the cloth itself. I implemented shaders for lighting, texture mapping, bump mapping, displacement, and mirror reflections. The high-stiffness run stood out to me because the cloth kept shaking and folding once the integration became unstable. I also found it interesting to see the same brick height map add detail to the shading and, with displacement, change the sphere's outline.

Part 1: Masses and springs

I connected the point masses with structural springs along rows and columns, shearing springs on the diagonals, and bending springs that span two grid steps.

The cloth in pinned2.json is pinned at two corners along one edge.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

I add external forces and Hooke's law spring forces for each mass, using 20% of ks for bending springs. For Verlet integration, I advance each free mass by its previous displacement times 1 - damping / 100 plus its acceleration times the square of the timestep. After collision checks, I bring springs stretched beyond 110% of their rest length back to that limit by moving their free endpoints.

I changed one setting at a time in pinned2.json, starting from ks = 5000, density = 150, and damping = 0.2%. The density in the simulator panel is one tenth of the scene value. At default settings, the cloth swings down from horizontal and settles into a smooth drape. With ks = 50, the cloth starts by falling much like the default, then stretches farther and hangs lower with more ripples near the pins. At ks = 500000, the integration becomes unstable at the chosen timestep, and the cloth keeps jerking and folding.

The initial acceleration due to gravity is the same at both densities. At rest, there is less sag with density = 50 and more with density = 450 than in the default run.

At damping = 0%, the cloth swings past its hanging position and keeps moving after the default run has settled. At damping = 1%, it descends more slowly and barely rebounds.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

For a sphere, I check the new position against the radius and project a point inside the sphere radially onto its surface. I move the mass a fraction 1 - friction of the way from its previous position to the surface point.

For a plane, I check the signed distances of the old and new positions for a crossing and offset the intersection toward the side the mass came from. I apply the same friction scaling between the previous position and the offset point. I skip pinned masses in both collision checks.

In sphere.json, the cloth wraps closest to the sphere and hangs lowest at ks = 500. The drape is looser at the default ks = 5000. At ks = 50000, the cloth spreads farther out and its corners hang higher.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

After each Verlet update, I group the point masses into 3D cells using a spatial hash.

For each free mass, I check for other points in the same cell that are closer than twice the cloth thickness. I compute a correction away from each nearby point by the missing separation, then average and scale those corrections before moving the mass.

In selfCollision.json, the cloth buckles at the bottom, piles up, and eventually spreads out across the plane.

At density = 10, the lower edge curls into a broad roll, while at density = 1500 there are more small folds packed together at the base. With ks = 50, I saw a low pile with sharper wrinkles than in the default run. At ks = 500000, the integration is unstable, and the sheet wrinkles as it oscillates above the plane.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program is a set of linked GPU stages for projecting and shading geometry. In the vertex stage, I project each vertex into clip space and pass along attributes such as normals and texture coordinates to be interpolated across the triangle. In the fragment stage, I use the interpolated attributes with uniforms such as the light position and textures to compute a color.

For Blinn–Phong shading, I add ambient, diffuse, and specular terms. I use 10% of the base color for ambient light everywhere on the surface. For diffuse light, I multiply the base color by the clamped dot product of the normal and light direction. For specular light, I raise the clamped dot product of the normal and the halfway vector between the light and view directions to the 32nd power and scale it by 0.5. I scale both diffuse and specular by the light intensity divided by the squared distance to the light.

I sampled the grayscale bedrock texture at the interpolated UV coordinates. For bump mapping, I use height differences between neighboring texels to perturb the normals used for shading. For displacement, I also move vertices along their normals by the sampled height times the height scale.

I used the same brick height map, texture_3.png, for both effects. In the mesh comparison, I used height scaling 0.2 at both resolutions and normal scaling 100 at 16 × 16 and 50 at 128 × 128. The brick detail is visible in both bump renders, and the 128 × 128 sphere has a rounder outline. The displaced outline has broad steps and flat sections at 16 × 16, with finer ridges at 128 × 128 where the height map is sampled more densely.
